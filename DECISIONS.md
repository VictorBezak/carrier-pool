3. (AGENT) Carrier ranking is implemented as a deterministic fit score, not an acceptance-probability model. The broker TMS feeds record bookings, rates, locations, appointment actuals, corrections, and carrier changes; they do not record which carriers were called, tendered, rejected, or unavailable. The score therefore prioritizes carriers to contact using observable historical fit: similar-lane familiarity, positioning/deadhead, shrunk price history, appointment reliability, recent relationship activity, customer affinity, and rate/fall-through stability.

4. (AGENT) Lane matching uses ZIP centroid distance kernels rather than city/state buckets. Direct lane evidence is weighted by origin and destination proximity; reverse-lane evidence is allowed but discounted. This avoids treating all TX-to-TX loads as equivalent while still letting Grand Prairie/Katy history inform Arlington/Sugar Land.

5. (AGENT) Small-sample carrier statistics are shrunk toward broker-local priors instead of trusting raw averages. Price uses a k=4 prior; reliability uses a four-observation Beta-style prior; customer affinity is heavily shrunk. Confidence is calculated separately from score so a high-fit carrier with thin evidence can be shown as low or medium confidence.

6. (AGENT) Tenant isolation is structural in the ranker: feature statistics are keyed by `(broker_id, carrier_id)`. MC/DOT is identity metadata only and is not used to blend performance across brokers. This preserves the user's shared-pool rule that an overlapping carrier remains evaluated from the requesting broker's own history.

16. (AGENT) Deadhead is now measured properly and weighted as heavily as lane familiarity. Investigating (15) turned up a mis-measurement as well as an under-weighting, so both were fixed.

    **What was wrong.** Deadhead lived in a component called `positioning` that was 70% "how far is this carrier's last recorded delivery from our pickup" and 30% "how often does this carrier start loads near our pickup". Four separate problems compounded:

    - *The empty miles were counted in absolute terms only.* 165 empty miles scored the same whether the load itself was 200 miles or 1,200. Those are completely different propositions: on a 209-mile load the carrier drives 374 miles to get paid for 209, and on a 1,200-mile load nobody notices.
    - *The 30% "starts loads near our pickup" term quietly refunded the penalty.* Worse, it was earned by the same historical loads that had already earned the carrier its lane-familiarity points, so one fact was paid for twice. Concretely: on the HaulDesk Seguin to Baytown load, Delta Prime's 165 empty miles should have cost it 9.1 points against a well-positioned truck, and the refund handed 5.5 of them straight back.
    - *Age was ignored.* A delivery that happened six days before our pickup was treated as authoritative evidence of where the truck is today.
    - *The component was worth 20% of the score* while lane familiarity was worth 28%, so on the load above the entire difference between 20 empty miles and 165 empty miles moved the ranking by 3.6 points out of 100 while knowing the lane moved it by 13.5.

    **The part that was not a bug.** The obvious reading — that we were recommending carriers with ruinous deadhead — turned out to be half wrong, and the reason matters. Delta Prime runs New Braunfels to Pasadena as a daily shuttle. Six days after its last Pasadena drop it is not sitting in Pasadena; it has cycled home to New Braunfels, 13 miles from the Seguin pickup. The "165 empty miles" the UI displayed was never a number Delta Prime would actually drive. The 30% density term existed as a crude patch for exactly this, which is why simply deleting it or turning the penalty up would have demoted every good shuttle carrier in the corpus — including Ibrahim Transport, the intended answer to the FreightFlow sanity load.

    **What replaced it.** `positioning` now estimates expected empty miles from two readings and blends them by how fresh the first one is:

    - *Last recorded delivery* — where a truck demonstrably was. Decisive when it is hours old, worthless when it is a week old. Freshness halves every 4 days.
    - *Operating footprint* — a recency-weighted soft minimum over every stop, pickups included, in the carrier's recent work. This is what catches the shuttle: a carrier that keeps originating loads near the pickup has equipment back there regardless of where it last dropped. A soft minimum rather than a hard one so a single ancient nearby stop cannot claim the carrier is parked there forever, and rather than an average so a genuinely close truck is not drowned out by the carrier's other work.

    The resulting mileage is scored on two scales — against a flat 45-mile allowance and against 15% of the load's paid miles — and judged by whichever is kinder, so a 30-mile drayage move is not punished for a large ratio and 165 miles on a long haul is not punished for a large absolute. The carrier's price evidence also now reports `all_in_ppm_with_deadhead`, what it actually earns per mile turned, since the quoted rate only pays for loaded miles; Delta Prime's $4.72/mi headline is $3.36/mi in practice, which is why a badly positioned carrier is the one most likely to decline or ask for more.

    **Honest limitations.** The footprint reading assumes a carrier with several recent loads has more than one truck, which is fair for the 8-load rotations in this corpus and unfair to a genuine owner-operator; real dispatch software would use equipment counts and truck-level availability, which no provided TMS schema carries. The 4-day freshness half-life, the 45-mile allowance and the 15% ratio are judgment calls chosen to be legible rather than fitted, since there is no acceptance or rejection data to fit them against (see (3)). And deadhead still does not feed the load's price estimate itself: that estimate answers "what is this lane worth", which should not change based on who we happen to be calling, so the deadhead-adjusted figure is reported per carrier instead of baked into the market price.

    Weights and the footprint's authority were corrected in (17) after this first pass under-delivered; the measurement design above is unchanged.

17. (AGENT) Geography is scored in exactly one place, and the inferred footprint cannot override an observed position. The first pass at (16) fixed the measurement but still ranked a carrier with 85 expected empty miles above one with 27, because two things were wrong with how the measurement was *used*.

    **The double count came back in a subtler form.** (16) pulled the pickup-density term out of the deadhead component and gave it its own scored line, `area_familiarity`. But the new operating footprint reads those same nearby pickups to cut the deadhead estimate. So Delta Prime's eight New Braunfels pickups were paid for twice again: once by dragging its estimate from 165 miles down to 85, and again as 8 points of `area_familiarity` — which is exactly the laundering (16) set out to remove, just one level deeper. `area_familiarity` is gone. Its numbers survive as `pickups_within_50mi` on the positioning component, where a broker can still audit them, but they no longer carry weight of their own. Geography is priced once.

    **The footprint was treated as an equal partner to an observed fact.** The last recorded delivery is something that happened; the footprint is an inference about a repositioning move nobody recorded. Worse, in this corpus Delta Prime has eight New Braunfels to Pasadena loads and zero in the reverse direction, so the return home it is being credited for is itself 165 unpaid empty miles it absorbs every cycle. Letting a stale drop decay to nothing meant that inference could erase the observation entirely. The footprint's influence is now capped at half (`POSITION_MAX_FOOTPRINT_WEIGHT`): it can pull the estimate halfway toward the carrier's usual area and no further, so a shuttle carrier still gets realistic credit for coming home while an observed position always keeps half the say. Delta Prime's estimate moves from 85 to 102 miles, the midpoint of the 165 it was seen at and the 40 it usually works within.

    **Weights.** `positioning` is now 0.30, the single heaviest component, ahead of `lane_familiarity` at 0.24. The README's framing is that carriers hate empty miles and a nearby truck is an easy yes, so empty miles outrank lane knowledge rather than merely matching it. The 0.08 released by dropping `area_familiarity` went to `positioning` and `relationship`.

    **Effect.** On the HaulDesk Seguin to Baytown load the ranking flips: Brazos, 27 empty miles and no corridor history, now leads Delta Prime 65 to 59. Waxahachie Way, sitting 4 miles from a BrokerOS pickup, goes from 2nd to 1st. 20. (AGENT) Positioning was the only component with no small-sample shrinkage, and the deadhead curve was deliberately left alone. Answering (19) meant deciding whether empty miles should outrank a carrier's track record; the conclusion is no, and the case that prompted the question turned out to be a missing shrinkage rather than a missing penalty.

    **Deadhead and broker margin point the same way, so there is less tension here than it looks.** A carrier prices empty miles into its rate. On the HaulDesk Seguin to Baytown load the well-positioned carriers are the cheaper ones — Brazos at $910 and Comal Creek at $938 against Delta Prime's $987 — because Delta Prime is quoting to cover a repositioning move the others do not have to make. Penalizing deadhead therefore protects the broker's spread as well as the carrier's margin. The place they genuinely diverge is a carrier who is close but unproven, which is what (19) is really about.

    **Why the curve was not tightened further.** At roughly $2.00/mile carrier operating cost, this load pays Comal Creek about $453 of margin over 243 miles driven and Delta Prime about $364 over 312. Delta Prime's version is roughly 20% worse for the carrier — real, but not disqualifying, and both clear cost comfortably. `positioning` already splits those two 30 points to 16, closer to 2:1, so the curve is if anything harsher than the underlying economics justify. Turning it up far enough to also outweigh Delta Prime's lane history, its 16-for-16 on-time record and eight loads of relationship would have meant recommending a carrier that was late on both of the only two appointments it has ever been measured on. That is not a defensible answer to "why did you call them first", and the ROI framing in (19) cuts against it too: a late pickup costs the broker a customer, not just a phone call.

    **The actual defect.** `positioning` shrank nothing, while `price` shrinks toward a k=4 prior and reliability uses a four-observation Beta prior (see (5)). So Comal Creek's single six-day-old Schertz delivery scored a flat 100 on the heaviest component in the model — identical to Brazos, whose 100 rests on eight consistent Schertz drops, the most recent two days old — while Comal Creek's own confidence badge read `LOW`. Freshness answers "is this position still true"; it cannot answer "is one drop enough to believe". Positioning now shrinks toward the unknown-position prior with a two-observation prior, so evidence depth and evidence age are handled separately. With zero observations the formula collapses to exactly the prior, so the unknown case stopped needing its own branch.

    **Effect.** Comal Creek falls from 3rd at 52 to 4th at 39 and Brazos keeps the load, 61 to Delta Prime's 58, so the broker's first call is now both the closest truck *and* a proven one. Waxahachie Way, one load and four empty miles, drops behind Pearland Produce Express, which knows that lane and that customer. Ibrahim still leads the sanity load at 71 with ten observations behind its estimate. A side effect worth naming: shrinkage caps what thin carriers can earn on positioning, so `Empty miles` now separates carriers by how well-evidenced their position is, not only by how close it is.

    **Reporting over prescribing.** Per (19), the ranking is a recommendation and the reasoning is the product. So the evidence now carries `position_observations`, the number of recorded deliveries behind the estimate, and a watch-out fires under three observations saying the carrier "may not have a truck free near the pickup". A broker who wants to gamble on the close unproven carrier can see exactly what they are gambling on, which is a better outcome than the ranker silently making that call either way.

    **Honest limitation.** A carrier with no delivery actuals scores the 0.35 prior, which beats a carrier we *know* is 228 empty miles out. That is correct expected-value reasoning rather than a bug — an unmeasured carrier should be scored at roughly the population average, and 0.35 sits slightly below the median position in this corpus — but it does mean absent data outranks bad data, and a broker could read that as the platform rewarding carriers it knows nothing about.
